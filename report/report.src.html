Wishing Tree

Kelly Enright · MGT 409, AI Foundations for Managers · Yale School of Management

Wishing Tree is a year-round giving tree that uses AI agents to help neighbors in New Haven ask for what they need, privately, and connects them with neighbors who want to help.

2Motivation

The giving tree. Growing up, my church hung paper ornaments with wishes from people in our community. A neighbor took one and made it come true. It worked beautifully — and I always wondered why only at Christmas. Need doesn't keep a calendar.

The winter drive. For seven years I volunteered at a soup kitchen, and every winter I organized the clothing drive — scarves, blankets, gloves, coats. People want to help the moment they know someone nearby needs it; willingness was never the problem. So many people needed help, so many people I knew wanted to give, and there was no bridge between them.

Lasagna Love. Matching neighbors with someone close by to cook them a meal showed me that local, personal giving works, all year round. I cooked for a single mother working several jobs, a family spending long days at the hospital while their child went through chemotherapy, parents stretching every dollar to feed their kids. And I saw the gap again trying to help a single mother of three facing eviction, with no simple, trusted way to help her directly.

Who has this problem, and what it costs them

This is not a thin slice of Connecticut. United Way of Connecticut's ALICE analysis found that in 2023 about 40% of Connecticut households — a record 581,000 — could not afford a basic survival budget, rising above 60% in cities including New Haven.

The cruelty is structural. ALICE households — Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed — earn above the federal poverty line, which is precisely what excludes them from most income-based aid. Working, over the threshold, and still short at the end of the month.

Source: CT Mirror, September 2025, reporting United Way of Connecticut's ALICE analysis for 2023.

What exists for them is seasonal and rationed. Salvation Army Angel Tree programs — the closest thing to what I'm describing — fill within hours: Augusta, Georgia closed in under 24, and Memphis filled in 10. Getting on the list means an in-person appointment with photo ID, birth certificates for each child, and proof of income. For a parent working two jobs without childcare, the appointment is the barrier, before the question of whether any slots are left.

Sources: WRDW, 2025 (Augusta); FOX13 Memphis, 2025 (Memphis).

So the cost falls twice: a family goes without a winter coat because they couldn't take a Tuesday morning off, and a neighbor who would gladly have bought it never hears about it.

What Wishing Tree does differently

  • Fairness by design. Every wish is rewritten into one neutral format before anyone sees it, and the tree ranks on urgency and how long a wish has waited — never on how movingly it was written. A neighbor who writes well should not get helped sooner than one who doesn't.
  • AI does the work that makes these programs hard to run. The intake interview and the privacy review are exactly the labor that forces other programs into appointments, caseworkers and partner nonprofits. Here they are two agents, and they cost a tenth of a cent per wish.
  • No forms, no appointment, no app. You type what would help in your own words, at 11pm if that is when you have a minute, on a website with nothing to download.
  • Privacy enforced in code, not just asked for. Contact details never reach any agent, and a public card is built by naming the fields a granter may see rather than removing the ones they may not.
  • Hyperlocal, physical, year-round. One drop-off hub, one weekly delivery run, hand-delivered, for every kind of need — coats, groceries, a bed frame, help with rent — not one category in one season.

Where we fall short, honestly. We have no partner verification yet. Every other program on that list has some check that a request is genuine — a nonprofit vouching, an in-person appointment, documents. We have caps, a safety agent and a human admin, which is not the same thing. It is the first item in the next steps for a reason: it is the gap between a working demo and something a community could safely rely on.

4The agents

Four design choices that matter more than the prompts

Code enforces what the agents are asked to do. A prompt is a request; code is a promise. Every rule the Safety agent is given is checked again in plain Python after it answers, and that second pass can only ever make a wish less public. If the agent skips a tool, the wish is still checked. The caps are checked on the server immediately before every model call, so nothing sent from a browser can get around them.

The list of personal details is closed. What a neighbor is told was taken out comes from a fixed vocabulary of ten phrases — a name, a child's name, a school name, an employer's name, a landlord's name, an address, a phone number, an email address, a social media handle, a medical detail. Anything else the agent claims it removed is dropped before the neighbor sees it, because the line reads "Taken out before anyone saw it" and that is a promise about their privacy.

Contact details never reach any agent. Addresses, phones and emails live in tables the agents cannot query; the Intake agent knows who it is talking to by alias only. A test checks every seeded person's real address and phone against the raw body of every endpoint an account can reach.

The neutral format is a fairness measure, not tidiness. Every published wish is one plain fragment — the item and what it has to match. No first person, no relationships, no circumstances. A neighbor should not have to explain their situation to a stranger to be helped, and the tree should not reward the best writer.

Why gpt-6-luna

  • Cost. A whole neighbor journey is $0.00133 — section 5 has the arithmetic.
  • Tools and structured output. All three agents return typed objects — a SafetyReview, a list of DraftWish — which is what lets code check the model's work instead of parsing prose.
  • Prompt caching. The system prompts are long and identical every call. With an explicit cache breakpoint, cached input is the largest bucket in every agent's row, at a tenth the price of fresh input.
  • Enough context, and no need for more. Our own experiment showed that handing the Matchmaker every open wish instead of 15 candidates changed neither the wishes chosen nor their order.

Experiment: does the Matchmaker need more candidates? Run at a limit of 15 and with no limit at all, on the same five wish granters: all five got the same five wishes in the same order, for 4% more cost with no limit ($0.000460 → $0.000480 average). The limit stays — the cost grows with the size of the tree while the answer does not.

Experiment: does capping output save money? Three arms, since output is the dearest bucket and a cap per reply is the obvious lever:

A cap of 700 does nothing — mean output is about 386 tokens, so it never binds, and the lower bill in that sample is run-to-run variance. That is the easy mistake: set a cap, see a lower number once, conclude the cap caused it.

A cap of 300 does bind, and breaks things: two of ten intakes failed outright, the structured reply never produced. The eight that finished were 38% cheaper — 38% of a tenth of a cent, against a one-in-five chance that a neighbor who has just typed out what their children need gets an error instead of a wish. The failures are not even free: the tokens were spent before the limit was hit.

The cap is a trap; the prompt is the lever. What actually keeps replies short is telling the Intake agent to ask only the follow-ups that change what a granter would buy. That shortens output without ever truncating it.

Admin time, not AI cost, is the binding constraint. At ten thousand users the model bill is about eleven dollars a month — less than one volunteer's phone plan. The same ten thousand users would need far more than six hours of someone's Saturday. Every scaling question for this project is a question about people and vans, not tokens. That is worth knowing before anyone decides where to spend effort.

6Safety

This app handles the single most sensitive thing a person can hand over: where they live and what they are short of. The risks below are the ones that would actually hurt someone, and each has a defense that does not depend on an agent behaving well.

RiskDefense
Prompt injection
a wish that tries to give the agent orders		 Wishes are wrapped in <wish_from_family> delimiters and treated as data. An instruction inside is a finding — flagged and reviewed anyway — not a request to consider.
Personal details leaking Two layers: the agent removes them, then plain Python checks again and can only make a wish less public. Cards name the fields a granter may see, so a new column cannot leak by default.
Cash requests and scams Flagged as cash_request or scam and held. Money never moves between two people: rent and utilities are paid to the landlord or provider.
The agent inventing a detail The hardest rule here. A missing size or deadline must be asked for or left empty, never guessed: a granter buying the wrong size because a model filled a blank is a real harm.
Overspending 20,000 tokens per session and 500,000 per day, checked server-side before every call. Neither is readable from the environment, so a deployment cannot loosen them.
Account security bcrypt at work factor 12, session tokens and reset codes stored as SHA-256 hashes, HttpOnly cookies, and a passcode on every screen that shows an address.

Bugs our own guardrails caught

The defenses were worth building partly because they caught our own mistakes, not only hypothetical attackers:

  • Safety reviews were being charged to the neighbor's chat budget. A review runs after the neighbor has approved and walked away, but its tokens were counted against their session cap — so a neighbor with several wishes could be cut off by work they were not doing.
  • A time-zone bug in "days waiting". Comparing a UTC timestamp to a local date made wishes look a day older or younger than they were, which fed straight into the Matchmaker's ranking.
  • A reworded wish reported as a privacy removal. The line "Taken out before anyone saw it: a use-purpose detail" was both untrue and meaningless. That is what the closed vocabulary in section 4 exists to prevent.

Two things in this build are demo-grade, and should not be mistaken for production security.

The admin passcode is a single shared secret with no rate limiting, no lockout and no individual accounts. Real staff access needs real accounts with real audit trails.

The "Continue as…" shortcut signs you in as any seeded account with one click. It exists so a grader can move between roles without typing, it is labeled as a demo shortcut in the app, and it has no place in anything real.

7Impact

Read the screenshots carefully. The "24 wishes granted so far" counter and the gold leaves on the tree come from invented demo data, seeded by backend/seed.py. Every neighbor, wish granter, address, phone number and wish in this project is made up. They demonstrate what the app does at a realistic size. They are not impact, and nothing in this report should be read as claiming a single real wish has been granted.

What we can already say

The intake is fast. Across 35 real intake sessions recorded in output/audit_trail.json, the model work takes a median of 9.8 seconds. End to end in the app — a person typing, reading the reply, checking the draft and posting — the two conversations in section 4 took 44 and 102 seconds, measured by the app itself and shown on the Agent activity page.

Set against what exists today, from section 2: an in-person appointment with photo ID, birth certificates and proof of income, for a program that may already have filled in ten hours. The comparison is a minute and a half at your kitchen table against a morning off work for a place in a queue that may be closed.

And the cost of carrying one neighbor through it is $0.0013 — which is the part that makes a year-round version plausible at all, because the intake and privacy work is exactly the labor that forces other programs into appointments and caseworkers.

How it would reach people

The two sides need different routes, which is the thing most giving platforms get wrong by treating them as one audience.

Wish granters come through social proof. Instagram, where a granted wish is a genuinely good post; student ambassadors; and the SOM community, which is both local and unusually willing to turn up on a Thursday afternoon.

Wishes come through trusted partners. Nobody in a hard month goes looking for a giving website. They do talk to their child's school, their church, and the pantry they already visit. Those partners are also the referrers who solve the verification gap — the same relationship does both jobs.

And it grows the way Lasagna Love grew: community leads who own a region, recruit their own granters and run their own drop-offs. That model scales on people who want to help, which is the one resource this problem has never been short of.
